Choosing the right tennis racket for your level

By Sasha AceApril 3, 20254 min read

Choosing a racket is a little like choosing a dance partner: it should work with your movement, not make you fight for every step. The most expensive frame, or the one your favourite professional uses, is not automatically the best match for you.

A useful racket lets you swing comfortably, find the ball consistently and stay in control as a session goes on. Start with your current game and physical comfort, rather than buying equipment for the player you hope to become years from now.

1. Match the racket to your experience

If you are beginning, look for a forgiving frame that is easy to manoeuvre. A somewhat larger head can give you more room for off-centre contact. Avoid assuming that the lightest possible racket is always the easiest: you still need enough stability for the ball you are receiving.

As your technique develops, you may prefer a balance of power and control. More experienced players sometimes choose a smaller head or a heavier frame, but these are preferences to test — not milestones that every improving player must follow.

2. Understand weight and balance together

The number on the label tells only part of the story. Two rackets with the same overall weight can feel very different when swung because of how that weight is distributed. Strings and an overgrip also change the finished setup.

  • Manoeuvrability: can you prepare comfortably for volleys and return a quick ball?
  • Stability: does the racket stay reasonably steady at contact?
  • Endurance: can you keep your normal swing late in a session without straining?

Test at an ordinary training pace, not just with a couple of impressive swings in a shop. A racket that feels great for five minutes may feel very different after a set.

3. Check the grip size

Your hand should wrap around the handle comfortably without needing a tight squeeze to keep control. A grip that is much too small can encourage unnecessary gripping; a much larger one may make the racket harder to manage.

Try adjacent sizes if possible. The familiar “finger gap” check is only a rough guide, because hand shapes and preferences differ. Remember that adding an overgrip increases the size slightly. Ask a coach or racket specialist to help if you are unsure.

Coach’s tip: Comfort is not a luxury feature. If a frame or string setup causes discomfort, do not assume you simply need to get used to it. Stop, review your equipment and technique, and seek professional advice for persistent pain.

4. Do not overlook the strings

Strings influence how a racket feels at contact. A comfortable synthetic gut or multifilament can be a sensible starting point for many recreational players. Stiffer polyester setups may suit some games, but are not a necessary upgrade for a beginner.

Stay within the manufacturer’s recommended tension range and discuss your needs with a qualified stringer. Change one variable at a time so you can tell whether an adjustment actually helps.

5. Demo before you decide

If a demo programme is available, compare two or three rackets during similar sessions. Hit groundstrokes, volleys, returns and serves. Include defensive shots and slightly late contacts, not only the balls you strike perfectly.

  1. Start with the same warm-up and familiar drills.
  2. Notice comfort, control and ease of preparation.
  3. Try each racket for long enough to judge fatigue.
  4. Choose the one that supports your ordinary game most consistently.

For children, use age- and size-appropriate equipment rather than a full-size adult racket chosen only for its appearance. Whatever your level, the right purchase should make practice more enjoyable — it cannot replace practice itself.
